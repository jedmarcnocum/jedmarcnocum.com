CML Lab Rebuild, Part 1: Getting Started with CML-Free

Why I’m rebuilding my CCNA lab

While studying for the CCNA, I used Cisco Packet Tracer alongside Jeremy’s IT Lab course on Udemy. As I worked through the lessons, the individual lab exercises sometimes felt disconnected because each focused on a specific topic. To see the bigger picture and connect the dots, I began building my own topology rather than treating every topic as a separate exercise. I expanded that topology as I learned more.

That project now covers VLAN segmentation, trunking, inter-VLAN routing, RSTP, EtherChannel, OSPF, HSRP, DHCP, DNS, NAT/PAT, ACLs, IPv4/IPv6, wireless, voice/QoS, and access-layer security controls. It gave me a solid foundation and helped me pass the CCNA, but I did not want the project to end after the exam.


I wanted to keep building in an environment closer to a Cisco lab workflow: creating nodes, wiring interfaces, opening device consoles, and troubleshooting the platform itself. That is why I decided to begin rebuilding the project in Cisco Modeling Labs (CML). Before committing to a paid CML subscription, I decided to begin with CML-Free. Its five-running-node limit was enough to test how CML fits my workflow, learn the platform, and confirm that I could build and troubleshoot Cisco labs comfortably in it.

Setting up CML in VMware Workstation

Setting up CML in VMware Workstation was straightforward. I downloaded the CML-Free OVA file, imported it into VMware, and configured the virtual machine’s CPU, memory, and network settings. After powering on the VM, I can access both cockpit and the CML web interface through a browser.

Getting into the CML interface

After completing the initial setup, I checked the CML controller in Cockpit. This confirmed that the Ubuntu virtual machine was healthy and gave me a view of its CPU and memory use, uptime, and virtual hardware configuration.

I then opened CML's Node Definitions page to see the device types available for lab topologies and to explore the options for adding or importing definitions. This was my starting point for becoming familiar with the platform before building the first test lab.

Next, I created a new CML lab and added an IOSv router and an IOSvL2 switch to the canvas. I linked their GigabitEthernet0/0 interfaces, started both nodes, and opened their consoles. Seeing both devices boot to the Cisco IOS CLI confirmed that the basic workflow was working.

The goal was not to recreate the whole CCNA topology yet. I wanted to verify the basic workflow first:

  • Add an IOSv router and IOSvL2 switch to a CML canvas.
  • Link their GigabitEthernet0/0 interfaces.
  • Start the nodes and open each console.
  • Confirm that the Cisco CLI is accessible.

First successful CML test

After creating the link, I started both nodes and opened their consoles. The CLI loaded successfully, confirming that the reference platform, node images, lab wiring, and console access were all working.

This small test was an important milestone: I had moved from an existing Packet Tracer portfolio to a working CML environment where I can rebuild the same design progressively.

What’s next

The next step is to rebuild a compact version of the existing core topology within CML-Free’s five-node limit, then upgrade to CML-Personal or CML-Enterprise to continue the rebuild. I will then progressively add more nodes and features, including:

  • VLANs and trunking.
  • EtherChannel and spanning-tree behaviour.
  • Inter-VLAN routing, OSPF, and HSRP.
  • DHCP, DNS, NAT, ACLs, and IPv6.
  • Troubleshooting and verification evidence.

Later, I plan to integrate my separate Windows Server and Active Directory lab in VirtualBox so I can practise cross-discipline troubleshooting — for example, determining whether a client issue is caused by VLAN assignment, DHCP, DNS, routing, ACLs, or Active Directory. I am also considering building scripting, automation, firewall and security labs in CML, as well as exploring the integration of CML with Ansible and other automation tools. This will allow me to expand my skills beyond traditional networking and into the realm of network automation and orchestration.

Why I’m documenting the build

This blog is part of my networking portfolio. I want to show the process behind the project: what I was trying to achieve, what went wrong, how I checked it, and how I verified the fix.

The original Packet Tracer project remains available on GitHub . The next post will cover the first switching stage and the design choices I make as I translate the existing lab into the new environment.
