🏗️ CML Lab Rebuild, Part 2: The Five-Node Foundation (and a Plot Twist!)

Hello there! 👋 Welcome back to Part 2 of the CML rebuild blog series. As planned, I’ve spun up five nodes in our CML lab, connected the links, and named the devices using a clean format: SITE-DEVICE_TYPE-NUMBER. 🚀

🤔 The Original Plan vs. The Plot Twist

I’ll admit, I was so excited to start labbing that I completely jumped the gun right after adding the nodes! My original plan was to just copy the exact same layout and VLAN scheme from my old CCNA lab and build a classic compact HQ with a collapsed core. That old lab was basically built like a Lego set — completely unplanned and snapped together piece by piece as I progressed through my lectures. I managed to build it, and while it wasn't perfect, hey, everyone starts somewhere! 🤷‍♂️

It was a very happy-go-lucky approach back then, and while it was great for getting my feet wet, I realized that for this rebuild, I need to actually plan in advance instead of just winging it. I didn't want to just copy-paste my past work; I wanted a genuine learning experience and enjoy the process. So, after taking a step back and doing some research on modern network topologies, I decided to rip up the original plan and pivot to a Routed Campus Design. 🔄

Instead of dealing with Spanning Tree (STP) blocking ports and stretching Layer 2 domains, we are pushing Layer 3 routing all the way down. STP still sticks around for local Layer 2 protection, but it's officially fired from its job of controlling upstream routed paths! 🛑 Instead, OSPF ECMP steps in to handle load balancing and failure resilience, so we can actually utilize all our uplink bandwidth simultaneously instead of having ports sitting idle in a blocking state.

Here is how our five nodes fit into this new, modernized architecture:

  • HQ-ASW-01 (Access): Instead of just Layer 2 VLAN trunks, this switch will now participate in routing, eliminating the need for STP to manage redundant uplinks.
  • HQ-DSW-01 & 02 (Distribution): These act as our routed core/distribution layer. No more HSRP or complex Layer 2 load balancing — we'll rely on OSPF ECMP!
  • HQ-EDGE-01 (Edge Router): The Layer 3 boundary between HQ and the simulated provider.
  • ISP-01 (ISP Router): An isolated provider simulation to test a proper external routing boundary.

Even though the logical design shifted halfway through, the physical topology and foundational setups remain exactly the same. 🧱

Rethinking the spanning-tree plan

After creating the VLANs and moving on to STP, I reviewed my CCNA lab topology to work out how I could reproduce the same steps here. My CCNA lab uses a traditional campus design, so I asked AI (thanks, AI!) to evaluate the topology, identify improvements, and show me other design options. That led me to the routed campus design.

Before configuring STP on the switches, I decided to learn how a routed design differs from my current topology. A routed campus reduces reliance on STP to solve upstream loop issues and on FHRP for gateway redundancy: routed links can use OSPF equal-cost multipath (ECMP) for load balancing and failure resilience. The VLANs and SVIs move to the access switches instead of staying on the distribution switches as they did in my old CCNA lab.

I had already configured the spanning-tree priorities in CML and started writing this section 😅, but no worries! I can revert the distribution-switch VLAN work with the matching no commands and retain the VLANs on the access switch. 😉

The block-of-100 method

After lots of coffee and chats with AI, I landed on the block-of-100 method for the new VLAN naming and subnetting plan. Instead of assigning VLAN IDs to HR, IT, and voice at random as the network grows, each access switch receives its own block of 100 VLAN IDs and a corresponding larger address allocation, such as a /16 or /20 supernet.

Leaving room to grow

The gap between VLAN 110 and VLAN 120 is intentional. HR starts with 192.168.110.0/24, which provides 254 usable addresses, while 192.168.111.0/24 through 192.168.119.0/24 remain reserved for HR expansion on the same switch. IT then owns the next block, .120 through .129, followed by voice at .130 through .139. Each service block can therefore grow across ten /24 VLANs - up to 2,540 usable addresses - without rewriting the addressing scheme.

This makes the schema predictable. If I see a syslog message or an address such as 192.168.210.45, I can immediately tell that it is an HR device on Access Switch 2 in VLAN 210, without checking a spreadsheet.

Design checks as the lab scales

  • Include a location anchor: Do not name a local Layer 3 switch VLAN only HR. Include the switch or floor identifier, such as AS1-HR or FL1-HR, so routing tables stay unambiguous.
  • Watch the octet limit: The third octet of an IPv4 address stops at 255. A direct VLAN-to-octet convention eventually needs a different plan as VLAN counts grow.
  • Plan for larger environments: Beyond 255 VLANs, use a /23 or /22 allocation per switch, or use a 10.BuildingID.VLAN.0/24 scheme that uses the last two octets for the VLAN ID.

We may not reach the 255-VLAN limit in this lab, but it is a useful scaling consideration to take into future work. :)

🚦 Getting Started with VLANs and Subnetting

Now that the routed architecture is officially decided, it was time to tackle the logical side: IP addressing.

Because VLANs and SVIs will now live right on the access switches with purely routed upstream links, my old CCNA addressing scheme wasn't going to cut it. I needed a clean, scalable subnetting plan that would play nicely with our new routed design and give this rebuild a much cleaner path forward. I will admit that what I've come up with is a chaotic combination of lots of research, and plenty of arguing with AI about why X is better than Y 😂 — but I'm honestly still proud of the outcome!

P.S.: Feel free to give me tips on how to improve this plan in the LinkedIn comments or PM me. I know it can be better, and I want to learn from you all! 🙏

How expansion works

The third octet reserves a function range rather than copying the VLAN ID forever. VLAN 30, HR-USERS, starts at 10.1.30.0/24.

When HR needs more capacity, create a new, separate HR user VLAN and subnet:

  • First expansion: 10.1.31.0/24
  • Further HR subnets: 10.1.32.0/24 through 10.1.39.0/24

Each remains its own VLAN and /24 subnet, so expansion stays predictable instead of cramming multiple networks into one VLAN. 🤝

Now for the fun (and sometimes intimidating) part: IPv6! 🌐 To keep us from going cross-eyed, the IPv6 site and VLAN structure mirrors IPv4 wherever practical. The hexadecimal values at the end of these IPv6 addresses do not mirror IPv4’s decimal host numbers. HQ uses the documentation prefix 2001:db8:1::/48.

IPv4 uses defined numerical host ranges for static, DHCP, and reserved addresses. IPv6 does not mirror those decimal ranges. Readable IPv6 host values are used only for selected static infrastructure, while client addresses are assigned dynamically through SLAAC and/or DHCPv6. DHCPv6 does not provide the default gateway; IPv6 clients learn the default router through Router Advertisements.

⚙️ Base Device Configuration

Before applying the shiny new routed design, I needed to establish a consistent device baseline across the topology.

Whether you are building a Layer 2 or Layer 3 network, this baseline is always identical. Before getting to the exciting routing stuff, we have to lock the doors. With the nodes linked up, I applied a consistent starting configuration to establish local administrative access, protect privileged mode, enable SSH, and kill unused HTTP services. 🔒

Cisco IOS baseline
ip domain name hq.example.test
! Lab baseline only: centralized AAA and management filtering come later
username admin privilege 15 secret <secret>
enable secret <secret>
crypto key generate rsa modulus 2048
ip ssh version 2
no ip http server
no ip http secure-server

! Timeouts are extended for labbing purposes
line console 0
exec-timeout 30 0

line vty 0 6
 login local
 transport input ssh
 exec-timeout 10 0

Each device gets its own secure secret values, while the shared baseline keeps our console sessions tidy. 🤫
