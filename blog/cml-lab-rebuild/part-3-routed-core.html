🔀 CML Lab Rebuild, Part 3: VLANs, OSPF & a Resilient Routed Core

Welcome back! In Part 2, I planned the five-node topology and the dual-stack addressing scheme. This time, the lab finally starts behaving like a network: VLAN services go live on the access switch, the routed links get their point-to-point addressing, and OSPF takes over the job of finding the best path. 🚀

The goal was deliberately simple: build each layer in a way that I can prove. Rather than stopping at a green topology diagram, I wanted gateway checks, neighbour adjacencies, routing-table evidence, and end-to-end pings for both IPv4 and IPv6.

🏢 HQ access services: VLANs and dual-stack gateways

I started at HQ-ASW-01, where the Site 1 VLANs become useful services rather than just rows in an address plan. Each SVI gives its VLAN an IPv4 and IPv6 default-gateway presence, while keeping the naming and addressing conventions from the previous post intact.

This is the point where the routed-access design starts to feel real. Endpoints will eventually use these SVIs as their first hop, but the switch itself can already use the dual-stack gateways to participate cleanly in the wider infrastructure.

🔗 Routed infrastructure: small prefixes, clear boundaries

Next came the infrastructure links. The point-to-point uplinks use IPv4 /31 and IPv6 /127 allocations: exactly two usable addresses per link and no wasted space. Loopbacks provide stable infrastructure identities for routing and troubleshooting, even if a physical path changes later.

I also kept the inter-switch bundle visible and verifiable. A neat design on paper is nice; checking the VLAN, LACP state, and port-channel addresses is what makes it something I can confidently build on.

On HQ-DSW-01 and HQ-DSW-02, VLAN 998 is created as the parking VLAN for unused ports.

Need a refresher before the routed links? Review the dual-stack endpoint allocation from Part 2.

🔀 Dynamic routing: OSPF for IPv4 and IPv6

With the interfaces addressed and reachable, OSPF was the fun part. I enabled the IPv4 and IPv6 processes across the routed infrastructure, advertised the loopbacks, and let each node learn the rest of the core dynamically. No static-route maze, no manual failover edits—just a routing protocol doing the bookkeeping.

The neighbour view is my first sanity check. If both protocol families form the expected adjacencies, the routing tables should have enough information to choose paths through either distribution switch.

This is the payoff for the routed-access pivot from Part 2. The routing tables show the learned OSPF routes, and equal-cost paths can be used through the routed core instead of leaving redundant uplinks idle behind a spanning-tree block. That gives the lab both useful bandwidth and a much cleaner failure story.

🧪 Validation: Dual-Stack Reachability and ECMP Path Selection

With OSPFv2 and OSPFv3 established, I validated reachability from HQ-ASW-01 to HQ-EDGE-01’s Loopback0. Successful IPv4 and IPv6 pings, together with the OSPF route tables, confirmed that the routed transit links, learned routes, and return paths were working end to end.

The traceroute test then made the routing decision visible. Responses were observed through both HQ-DSW-01 and HQ-DSW-02, confirming that OSPF had installed two equal-cost paths to the edge. The simulation below illustrates the same behaviour: separate traceroute probes can be forwarded through either distribution switch on their way to the destination.

🛡️ ECMP resiliency: proving the path survives a failure

ECMP is most useful when it is more than two healthy lines in a routing table. To test the failure path, I took HQ-DSW-01 out of service and watched OSPF remove its links from the topology.

The IPv6 traceroute from HQ-ASW-01 to HQ-EDGE-01 still completed through HQ-DSW-02. That is the practical result I wanted: the core degrades to one path, but reachability stays intact without a manual route change.

Validation: LACP Member Resiliency and an Unexpected Lab Behaviour

This check asked a practical question: if one cable inside the inter-distribution bundle is removed, can the rest of the lab keep talking? The answer from the controlled test was yes—the logical routed link stayed online and the access switch kept reaching HQ-DSW-02.

HQ-DSW-01 and HQ-DSW-02 are joined by Port-channel12, a Layer-3 LACP EtherChannel built from GigabitEthernet0/1 and GigabitEthernet0/2. OSPFv2 and OSPFv3 run on that logical port-channel, not on its individual physical members. I used a continuous ping from HQ-ASW-01 to HQ-DSW-02 Loopback0 (10.255.1.2) to observe the result.

I began with the CML GUI. First, I stopped the link, but the affected Port-channel member still showed as bundled (P). I then used the GUI to disconnect the link: the interface status became down, yet the Po12 member still remained (P) on HQ-DSW-02. In other words, the virtual link looked unavailable while the remote switch had not immediately removed it from the bundle.

That stale state meant HQ-DSW-02 could still send traffic—including OSPF Hellos—toward an unavailable member until the OSPF adjacency reached its dead timer and reconverged.

The earlier 88% ping result therefore showed loss, not seamless resiliency, and is not suitable evidence of a clean member failover. This is an observed CML/IOSv emulation behaviour during a one-sided administrative shutdown, not evidence that the Layer-3 port-channel or OSPF configuration was wrong. On physical hardware, a genuine single-link failure normally signals down at both ends and the remaining member continues carrying the bundle.

I therefore ran the continuous ping again while manually shutting down the same physical member—GigabitEthernet0/2—on both distribution switches. Both devices immediately agreed on the active bundle membership, while Port-channel12 remained operational over the remaining member.

What this proves

Port-channel12 is resilient to the controlled removal of one member: the logical routed link remains up, OSPF stays established, and the access switch retains reachability to HQ-DSW-02. The one-sided test also highlighted an emulation edge case worth recognising when interpreting virtual-lab results.

📋 Part 3 summary

Part 3 turned the planned topology into a working dual-stack routed campus core:

  • Created the Site 1 VLAN services and dual-stack gateway SVIs on HQ-ASW-01.
  • Applied the compact point-to-point addressing approach to the routed infrastructure and checked the inter-switch bundle.
  • Formed OSPF adjacencies for IPv4 and IPv6, then verified learned routes and end-to-end reachability.
  • Set the stage for ECMP-capable paths through the core—the big reason for choosing routed access in the first place.
